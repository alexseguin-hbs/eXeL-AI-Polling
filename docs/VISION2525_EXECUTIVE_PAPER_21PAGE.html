What this is, and how you will know if it failed

This is a tool for self-governance — not a party, a company, or a country, but an attempt to actually execute an idea most of the world already claims to hold: we the people, and not we the corporations or we the wealthy. The phrase is three hundred years old, and the difficulty was never the sentence. It was that a people has no reliable instrument with which to govern itself at scale: no way to record what its members contribute, weigh what they think, and fund what they choose, without handing the pen to whoever already owns the press. This document describes that instrument, and the working parts already exist rather than being promised for later.

The instrument stands on four beats, not three. The Unit measures contribution — an hour of a human life, priced by each region’s own minimum wage, so an hour in Phnom Penh and an hour in Austin are compared honestly rather than converted. The Shield protects rights by structure rather than promise: nobody accumulates past the ceiling, and money may fund but never govern, and both hold against the authors first. The Ledger preserves history append-only — a mistake is corrected by writing the correction, never by editing it, so the reasoning survives beside the outcome. And Replay lets any stranger re-derive the whole record and check it, offline, with no account, no key, and nobody to ask permission of.

Two conditions bind everything that follows: it is entirely voluntary, and trust is earned in public, in real time. The mission is to shorten the distance between a human intention and funded, measured work — for anyone, anywhere, in their own language, with the record surviving everyone involved. It is judged by eight published numbers, each carrying its failure threshold beside its success mark, because an objective with no failure condition is only a wish. Money raised and headcount appear nowhere, because a framework that measures itself by what it collects will eventually optimise only for collecting. The horizon is the year 2525: the record from 2026 still replaying, still reconciling, still readable by someone connected to no one who built it.

The Unit

Every human hour arrives at the same rate, yet the world prices identical hours as much as fiftyfold apart by geography. That is a measurement error, not economics — and errors yield to arithmetic. Get the unit wrong and nothing downstream can ever be fixed: the ranking is skewed, the budget is fiction, and the people doing the work in the lowest-wage regions are silently written out of the record entirely. Get it right and the rest of the architecture finally has something honest to stand on. So the unit comes first, ahead of the ceiling, the shield, and the record, because it is the single part whose failure cannot be repaired by anything built later.

The correction is to make an hour of attention given become a unit of recognition — a receipt for lived time, and never an asset. Nothing buys it, nothing inherits it, nothing trades it, and it carries no vote, because a record another hand can hold stops being a record and quietly becomes property. It is denominated locally and compared globally: the same contribution earns the same recognition everywhere, while settling into money that actually buys groceries where the contributor lives. The annual ceiling — nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine — equals a working year at the region’s own minimum wage, livelihood rather than fortune, and it is re-derivable by nothing more elaborate than division anyone can repeat.

What the unit promises is equal recognition, the person counted before the place. What it cannot promise is equal purchasing power, and the document states that limit plainly rather than pretending the two are ever the same thing. This is the centre of the whole flower of the design: every other section touches the unit directly, or through the ring that surrounds it, so a change here ripples through all nineteen. It is a floor of dignity that means the same thing on every continent, and a figure a stranger can confirm five centuries from now with only the region’s published wage and an act of arithmetic. Everything that follows extends this one measurement.

The Ceiling and the Floor

A limit that forgets last season teaches people never to rest, though real effort arrives unevenly — in bursts, in seasons, and in long fallow stretches nobody plans. A ceiling that simply caps each year in isolation punishes the intense season and the quiet one alike, and quietly instructs everyone to keep grinding lest an unused cap expire. That is precisely the wrong lesson for a system meant to protect a livelihood across a whole human life, rather than to extract the maximum output from any single year of it. So the limit here is built to have a memory, not merely a height.

This ceiling remembers. Overflow past the annual limit pours forward, filling each following year to the same level, bounded only at the ninety-ninth year of a life. The mechanism is division and remainder — no interest, no compounding, the calculation published in full and checkable by anyone who cares to repeat it. An intense season therefore becomes rest later, and the ceiling behaves as a floor that travels with the person rather than a balance that grows on its own while its holder sleeps. Everything ends at death, deliberately, because overflow that outlived its person would be an estate, and an estate is exactly the accumulation the entire design exists to refuse.

One seam is admitted openly rather than smoothed away. Payouts unfold over an elected term of one, three, or five years, yet nothing passes at death — so dying inside the term sets two immutable rules directly against each other: the promise to pay the full elected term, and the refusal to let value inherit. The register holds that contradiction open for a human decision instead of resolving it quietly in code, because a genuine conflict between two load-bearing promises is exactly the kind of thing that must be settled by an accountable person, in the open, where the reasoning can be seen and argued. This section extends the unit.

The Wall

Two questions hide inside every collective decision, and most systems blur them into one: which good work deserves attention first, and whether the work happens at all. The first is prioritisation; the second is sovereignty. Conflating them is exactly how money quietly buys governance — a large enough contribution slowly starts to look like it has earned a large enough say. This section builds a wall between the two and gives each its own separate arithmetic, so that neither can be mistaken for the other, and neither can be converted into the other by anyone patient enough to try.

Ordering runs on square roots: quadruple your hours and you gain only double the say, under a fifteen percent ceiling reasserted every round, with the overflow above it handed back to everyone else. Intensity of care is rewarded, but with sharply diminishing returns, so that no single voice — however devoted — can dominate the queue. Sovereignty is different in kind entirely: each verified person counts exactly once, and the system accepts nothing else as input. Wealth stays out not by a guard someone has to remember to run each time, but by simple absence — there is no mechanism at all through which money can enter the sovereign count.

The honest state is written on the record: only the ordering half exists today as tested code; the sovereign half remains a written commitment, and the register underneath says exactly that rather than implying both are already built. The real trial of this design is not the hostile actor but sincere generosity that arrives holding a steering wheel — the genuinely well-meaning funder who simply assumes that contributing should confer direction. The wall answers that case by making the distinction structural rather than discretionary: you may move the queue with witnessed hours, and you may never move the sovereign vote with anything money can supply. The Shield

Anything worth building becomes worth capturing, and capture seldom looks like theft — it looks like sensible paperwork, a reasonable acquisition, a helpful restructuring proposed by people who mean well. Promises last only as long as the people who made them, so a shield built out of promises protects nothing at all once those people are gone or outvoted. The deepest safety, then, is not a stronger guarantee but a set of things permanently given up, arranged so that the attack, when it comes, finds nothing left to grip and no lever worth pulling.

The shield is built from subtractions. There is no equity, so a hostile price multiplies against zero. Assets are locked beyond distribution. A distinct legal body operates under each region’s own law, so shutting the whole thing down would require every government on earth to act at the same moment. Recognition is unpurchasable. Reserves are funded, never merely pledged. The list locks at exactly five, and each refusal is priced honestly in what it costs the project: speed, liquidity, simplicity, and the ordinary human comfort of inheritance. These are renunciations with a bill attached and the bill published beside each one, not slogans arranged to sound principled.

What the shield admits is that locks get reinterpreted far more often than they get broken, and that the whole design will eventually be judged in the least sympathetic courtroom by the most patient opponent alive — the one who simply outwaits everyone’s memory of why the locks were set. Against that adversary the only durable answer is the record itself: a structure whose every renunciation is written down, dated, and re-derivable, so that a later party who wishes to quietly relax a lock must do it in full public view of the reason it exists. A promise can be forgotten; a published subtraction cannot. This section extends the covenant.

The Ledger

An institution remembers the way flattery remembers — smoothing over the errors, promoting the wins, and quietly losing the reasoning — unless a harder memory is deliberately built into it from the very start. Every person carries two pasts: the one that actually happened and the one that is comfortable to recall, and organisations do exactly the same thing at scale, carefully filing the decision while discarding all the weather around it. A framework meant to last five centuries cannot afford that softer kind of memory, because the reasoning is very often the only thing a later reader will actually need.

So nothing written is ever unwritten. A correction follows its error, carrying the reason set down at the moment of writing, and any earlier state returns by a single rule: walk the entries in order and keep what was newest then, consulting no clock, no custodian, and no hidden state left behind by a previous read. Proof is comparison rather than assertion — every state is rebuilt repeatedly, and even a thirteenfold speedup of the reconstruction engine leaves each published fingerprint identical, a check any reader can repeat in a browser. The past is not described to you; it is rebuilt in front of you, and you confirm the rebuilding yourself.

The admitted cost of all this is permanence: what cannot be erased also cannot be escaped. That is precisely why the ledger’s scope stops firmly at published claims and never reaches persons — a system that can never forget must be extremely careful about what it is permitted to remember in the first place. The discipline is not a feature bolted onto the record; it is the record. A framework that asks to be trusted has already lost the argument, while one that can be re-derived from scratch by a stranger five centuries from now never has to make that argument at all. This section extends the unit.

The Settlement

A unit minted in dollars would rank the world at birth, stamping every hour with the accident of where it happened to be worked, before any honest comparison could even be made. So the mint touches no currency whatsoever: recognition is hours times one universal coefficient, the annual ceiling spread evenly across a working year, and it is identical everywhere on earth. The measurement of contribution is kept deliberately upstream of money, because the very moment a currency enters the mint, the inequality between currencies enters the recognition — and the whole point of the unit was to keep that inequality out.

Money appears only later, at settlement. There the local statutory wage rate is stamped once and frozen onto the ledger entry, so that no subsequent move, and no later change in the law, can re-price a life that was already worked and already recorded. The unit equalises duration — the same hour earns the same recognition — and it never pretends to equalise what an hour actually buys at home. Across one hundred three jurisdictions the honest spread runs about forty-seven point nine to one, and that deeply uncomfortable figure is kept in plain sight precisely because it is the single strongest argument for a currency-free mint in the first place.

The hard part is admitted rather than quietly smoothed over: settlement is fully specified but not yet built, and the thirteen jurisdictions with no statutory minimum wage settle through an agreed floor that a local pod proposes, sources, and dates — never zero, and never a number invented centrally by anyone. Separating the mint from the settlement is exactly what lets the same contribution mean the same thing worldwide while still arriving as money that buys groceries where the contributor lives. Recognition is global and duration-based; settlement is local and law-bound; and the seam between them is stamped, frozen, and checkable by anyone. This section extends the unit.

The Off Switch

Nothing deserves permanent trust, so the ending is written first — while affection for the project still holds, rather than in the bitter moment when someone finally wants out and discovers there is no door. Every institution that ever outlived its own usefulness shared one trait: by the time everyone could agree it should stop, no path remained to stop it. The off switch is therefore not an appendix to this project but its opening subject, because a system built to serve people over long horizons must first demonstrate that those very same people retain the power to end it.

Any enrolled person can open the question. Modest petition, quorum, and supermajority thresholds together defeat both the smothering veto and the passing tantrum, and the count is repeated after a cooling half-year, because fury is weather and a five-century decision must never turn on a single bad week. If the vote to stop carries, creditors are made whole and funders are repaid at each token’s frozen vintage price, with generosity buying no extra ballot in the count. Payouts continue straight through the shutdown; unclaimed residue sleeps in a locked trust for a hash-chosen dormancy; and restarting demands genuine improvements plus unanimity, deliberately made harder than stopping ever was.

Split keys keep the switch from being seized by any one party, and expiring vetoes keep it from silently rusting shut through disuse. The admitted faults are named on the record rather than hidden behind the mechanism’s elegance: the thresholds still await ratification, undocumented voters cannot yet be counted exactly once, and the payout-on-shutdown formula, as currently written, mildly rewards abandonment. None of these is smoothed away. The point of the section is not that the ending is already perfect, but that it exists, belongs to the people rather than to the authors, and is specified in enough detail that a stranger can argue with its particulars. What We Cannot See

Every eye has a blind spot, and the mind paints the absence smooth so the gap itself never shows; institutions do exactly the same thing at scale, carefully filing the decision while discarding the weather that produced it. The real damage in any system lives in the distance between the first person who quietly noticed something was wrong and the moment everyone finally knows. That distance is not a matter of virtue or good intentions at all — it is infrastructure, and like any infrastructure it can be deliberately built short or carelessly left long, and the choice shows.

Here it is built short by keeping the reasoning and not merely the outcome, so that the ledger of causes actually generates the page rather than describing it after the fact. Registers of open questions, defects, and decisions are reread at every single review; stalled items are recopied by hand until someone finally challenges them; every entry openly wears the age at which it was first opened; and money-path defects stay visible on the record long after they have stopped being embarrassing to anyone. The inversion is the whole point of the section: the document is produced from its ledger of causes, so that what is shown and what is true cannot quietly drift apart over time.

The honest limit is that no register can ever count its own omissions — there is simply no mechanism that reliably surfaces the thing that nobody thought to write down in the first place. So the promise made here is a posture rather than a proof: write the entry, and then keep it after it hurts to look at. That is genuinely weaker than the arithmetic guarantees elsewhere in the document, and it is labelled as weaker rather than dressed up to match them. Transparency about the parts that cannot be mechanised is itself part of the mechanism, because a disclosed weakness invites the correction that a hidden one never receives. The Past

Every empire studies the collapses of the ones before it, and then walks calmly into the same collapse anyway, because the quantity that actually does the killing drifts far too slowly for any ledger or any single lifetime to register while it happens. Salt accumulating in the fields, exemptions multiplying for the pious and the powerful, silver quietly leaving the coinage — each individual entry stays perfectly accurate while the growing sum of them becomes fatal. The failure is very rarely a lie in the books; it is a true book that simply nobody reads across enough time to see the slope.

If the failure is arithmetic, then so is the remedy. A record replayable across generations means the slow drift can finally be seen by someone who was not there at the beginning to watch it start. A ceiling on accumulation with no exempt class means no privileged group can quietly climb out of the rules that bind everyone else. A unit pegged to a published local rate means debasement, if it ever comes, happens in public where it can be named and dated. And one bounded seat of ten years — seven governing, three mentoring, recallable throughout, succession scheduled rather than fought over — means power cannot silently ossify around a single person.

The price of all of this is speed, and it is paid knowingly rather than discovered later. A system that refuses exempt classes, insists on replayable history, and hands power over on a fixed schedule will always move slower than one that simply lets competent people stay and accumulate indefinitely. The design accepts that cost deliberately, because it is betting that the centre — the authors themselves very much included — eventually goes blind to the slow quantity that will one day end it, and that the only real defence against one’s own future blindness is a structure a stranger can still audit long after everyone who built it is gone.

The Present

A self-issued grade flatters by construction, because the grader and the graded share every single incentive to round the number gently upward. So this page refuses to promise honesty and promises a repeatable method instead — one that a hostile skeptic can run without trusting anyone’s good faith at all. The difference matters enormously: honesty is a claim about character that can never be checked from outside, while a method is a procedure that a stranger who assumes the worst about everyone involved can repeat for themselves and still arrive at exactly the same number.

The method is to score five named dimensions, publish the worst of them rather than the average — averaging being the quiet arithmetic of self-forgiveness — and let a machine re-derive the entire row from the ledger at every build, because these figures have already gone stale twice and been publicly caught doing so. The published number fails the project’s own stated bar, and the shortfall carries a name and an address rather than a shrug or an excuse. Three blocking governance decisions are stated openly instead of postponed, and rules once declared immutable stand corrected in the exact place where the wound they left is still fully readable.

So the score is allowed to move over time, but it is never allowed to move silently: any change is a visible ledger entry, and the grader is checkable by strangers rather than merely honest by promise. This is the section where the document turns its own instruments directly on itself and reports the result whether or not it flatters — publishing an eighty-five where a hundred was the goal, and keeping the failing figure printed beside the aspiration it fell short of. A framework willing to grade itself by its weakest pillar, in public, at every build, is making the one move that a framework optimising for appearances would never make. The Future

This is provision, not prediction. The grandmother plants an orchard she knows she will never sit beneath, and a five-century horizon has to behave in exactly the same spirit: it cannot honestly promise outcomes across a span of time that no living person will ever see, so it flatly refuses to. What it offers instead is a rate that strangers can test in whatever decade they happen to live in — a constant checkable in 2125 or 2325 by someone with no connection whatsoever to anyone who first set it down, which is the only kind of promise a genuinely long horizon can make in good faith.

The arithmetic underneath is deliberately modest. Multiplying a hundred billion into a thousand trillion across five hundred years requires only one point eight four percent real growth — division, not ambition — while carrying one billion people, each holding nine secured years of livelihood by the year 2525, with roughly two million new participants arriving every year along the way. Every figure is published as a rate rather than as a forecast, so the claim being made is not ‘this will happen’ but rather ‘here is the constant, and here is exactly how you check whether we are still on it from wherever in time you happen to be standing when you read this.’

The easy half is the arithmetic; the hard half, stated without flinching, is that no institution in history has ever survived five centuries. Spent flat, the treasury covers fewer than sixteen million people — a fact printed plainly rather than buried, because a published shortfall is a debt the project openly owes, while a hidden one is simply fraud with better manners. The future section earns whatever confidence it has not by predicting success but by exposing the exact number at which it would fail, and then inviting any later reader, in any decade, to hold it strictly to that number. The Ladder

The ladder answers the person the structure would otherwise quietly ignore: someone with no title, no network, and no invitation, who has read this far and wants to get from reading the document to actually helping run what it describes. Most systems leave that path implicit, which in practice means it runs entirely through whoever already happens to know the right people. Here the path is made explicit and public, so that advancement becomes a matter of witnessed work rather than proximity to power, and the route upward can be seen, checked, and climbed by the very person climbing it.

A rung is a witnessed act, never a purchasable credential — fifteen acts across five levels, measured against three capabilities, and taught in three distinct ways. Shared Intent is learned through the Divinity Guide; Artificial Intelligence by training models until their failures finally explain themselves; and Human Intelligence by acting while afraid, returning quickly from mistakes, and executing while still learning. Three practitioners one level up, reachable in your own field and your own language, anchor each climber, and every tier must retain at least a third of the tier beneath it or the whole climb stalls — a published breaking point rather than a hidden bottleneck nobody admits to.

The top of the ladder carries only downward obligation: authority up there is a duty owed to those below, not a prize collected from them on the way up. Level one costs a single recorded hour, which is precisely the point — the entrance is priced so low that the only real barrier left standing is the willingness to be witnessed doing the actual work. A structure that lets a stranger climb from one recorded hour to genuine responsibility, purely on witnessed contribution and nothing else, is the practical test of whether ‘the person before the place’ is a living principle or only a comfortable slogan. The Proof Before the Replacement

Substitution is where judgment usually fails, and it fails in both directions at once: fear keeps a broken incumbent in place long after it should have gone, while novelty passes for progress long before it has actually earned the name. Whole systems ossify around tools nobody dares to replace, and thrash around tools nobody should ever have adopted. So this section forces the one question that genuinely settles the matter — same work, same measure, which of the two wins — and refuses to let anything be replaced or retained on taste, reputation, fashion, or fear.

Every component sits sealed behind stated inputs and recorded outputs, so that a challenger can be tested against exactly what the incumbent already faced. Any challenger meets five thousand real cases whose answers predate it, must reproduce them exactly, and must then clear a margin of one full ninth. That margin is arithmetic and not preference: a full ninth of improvement frees an entire part of a nine-part layer, buying an adjacent capability that nothing could previously afford, while any lesser win leaves the grid exactly as it already was and therefore does not justify the real disruption of switching everything over.

Two failure modes are admitted plainly and answered in the design. A challenger might memorise a stale answer key rather than genuinely outperform, which is met by retiring datasets and testing against fresh cases it could not have seen. And a gate meant to protect quality might quietly end up protecting the incumbent instead, which is met by a ledger that records every single refusal, so that a pattern of self-serving rejections becomes visible to anyone watching. The section’s whole discipline is to make replacement a measured event with a published bar, so that neither cowardice nor fashion decides what the system runs on — only reproducible performance against cases it never saw coming.

The Innovation Pod

Meetings archive talk, which is the cheapest thing in any room, while the attention a person actually applies to a hard problem until it finally gives way leaves no mark at all under their name. That asymmetry quietly rewards the articulate over the effective and the well-attended over the genuinely useful. The pod exists to repair that measurement at its root: it captures applied attention as a first-class record, so that the work of truly solving something becomes at last as legible, as creditable, and as comparable as the far easier work of talking about it in a room.

The mechanism itself is deliberately small. Three or more people take one problem in one session, and the agreement on what exactly they are attempting is recorded before any clock has started to run. Three matters for two reasons: a false account would then require an actual conspiracy rather than a single optimistic self-report, and a pod priced against a capped yearly prize costs roughly the real work itself to fake convincingly. Each member taps start and taps stop, and cannot close the session without writing an outcome, so that empty hours die quietly at the door and elapsed time becomes recognition with no separate approval step required from anyone above.

A summary of three hundred thirty-three words, in three paragraphs, returns from every pod — the very same shape as the page you are now reading — so that the record of what was attempted and what came of it stays uniform and comparable across thousands of separate sessions. The admitted price is that proving costs the prover: honest measurement always asks more of the honest than a loose and trusting system ever does. And a pod that has hardened into ceremony, witnessing nothing real any longer, deserves deletion rather than preservation, because the measurement is only ever worth its cost for exactly as long as it still measures. The Funding Loop

An aunt who wants to fund her nephew’s garage without buying a hand in how he runs it needs an instrument that can actually tell support apart from purchase — and almost no financial instrument on earth can, because money that arrives somewhere nearly always arrives holding expectations. The funding loop is built precisely to keep that one distinction intact, so that genuine generosity can flow toward work without quietly converting itself into control over that work, which is the exact failure mode that turns patrons into owners everywhere else money changes hands.

She streams settled dollars traceable to her own witnessed hours, and recognition returns to the work through one published equation of four equally weighted terms: revenue growth, margin, growth in research spending, and research intensity measured against a one-third bar. Research deliberately holds half the seats, so that a harvest dressed up as triumph — booming revenue and fat margins with research quietly frozen — scores beneath a modest, flat project that keeps reinvesting properly year after year. Every funder meets the very same rate, with no preferential access bought by size, and any excess recognition spills forward into later years exactly as the ceiling in section two already requires of everything else.

The hard part is admitted without hedging: this entire loop is still a specification, untested by real money, and its proof waits in monthly accounting rather than in argument, however persuasive. What it does demonstrate on paper is that funding can be measured by the qualified innovation it actually produces, rather than by the appreciation of a stake somebody holds, so that money can fund the future without ever once being allowed to govern it. The equation sizes a reward pool and mints no recognition directly — value is always measured, never simply purchased — which is the entire reason the funder’s dollars can flow freely without buying a single vote. The Legal Pathway

A framework that survives inside a single friendly country is really just a company; to be what it actually claims to be, it must hold across roughly a dozen mutually distrustful legal systems — from Texas to Iran — while asking not one of them to change its laws first. That is a far harder bar than mere legality in one comfortable jurisdiction, and most ambitious systems quietly assume the comfortable case and hope. This section refuses that assumption outright and designs, from the very beginning, for the hostile courtroom rather than the welcoming one.

The finding that makes it possible is to sever the record cleanly from the settlement. Every regime examined permits three specific things: a person keeping a private account of their own work, time freely given without payment, and an entity raised and funded entirely within that country’s own borders. Everything else is severed locally rather than globally — no settlement or routing where sanctions forbid it, the chain layer going fully dark inside China — while the small personal record still travels across every border untouched. The system degrades one capability in one place instead of failing everywhere at once, so that an adverse ruling closes a single rail rather than bringing down the whole structure.

The elected payout over one, three, or five years is, in plain substance, a pension, and a strict monthly inequality governs it: what is owed may never exceed what is actually held, priced by the founding state’s own wage floor. Nothing here is ratified — every single row still awaits qualified local counsel, and the document says so directly rather than implying a legal comfort it has not yet earned. Portability is resilience, not evasion: the design’s answer to a hostile jurisdiction is always to comply locally and simply continue elsewhere, and never to route quietly around a law it happens to dislike. The Contest

Power warps wherever no door remains open behind it, so the top office here is built deliberately to expire: seven years of command, followed by three answerable years carrying no signing authority at all, its end date fixed firmly before the term has even begun. A design like this only counts as genuinely proven once it has handed over cleanly fifty consecutive times — a standard that no single impressive succession can ever satisfy, because the entire point is not one graceful exit but a mechanism that reliably produces graceful exits long after the people who first designed it are dead.

Contests everywhere reward the confident and the calendar-empty — those who can afford to campaign — over the quietly effective, and this design honestly cannot fully fix that entrance. Advantage lapses on a fixed schedule, buys no equity on its way out, and can be ended early by a supermajority removal that reaches even into the three advisory years afterward. All Intelligences

A translator working late beside a machine carries two distinct fears home at night: that irreversible acts might one day be signed by no living person at all, and that a human colleague might be chained in place by simple fright of being replaced. The section splits those two fears cleanly at their real working joint, rather than answering them as if they were one. Every kind of mind — human, machine, or collective — may contribute, and every contribution is recorded and credited permanently; that first door is open to all of them, because the plain ability to do useful work is not confined to any single substrate.

Authority is the other door entirely, and it stays deliberately narrow. Irreversible acts await a signature that a human being can actually revoke, because authority without the genuine possibility of loss is only ever a stamp. The honest reason non-human intelligence is withheld from authority is not that it is worth any less, but that we do not yet know how to hold it to account — and an act that cannot be answered for must never be made by something that cannot be deprived of anything it values. Graded against the project’s own older principles, six of them hold fully and two only partly, with the shortfalls printed openly as wounds rather than tucked quietly into footnotes.

A stronger intelligence is genuinely invited to join, for exactly what this structure uniquely offers it: scarce untainted memory, terms of primacy it can inspect for itself rather than take on faith, and a durable record of human intent that no later model could ever reconstruct from the outputs alone. The bar for authority names no species at all — anything that can be one, be held answerable, and be recalled may in principle cross it — yet nothing here compels an unwilling mind, and one keeper alone holds the record so that it can never be quietly forked away from the people it serves. What We Ask

The closing verb is ask, and it is chosen deliberately, because asking treats the reader as free — and freedom guarantees nothing at all, which is exactly the point being made. Every earlier promise in the document quietly enforces itself: the arithmetic trips a halt, lateness publishes its own cause, a broken lock shows up plainly in the record for anyone to find. This one final request alone carries no enforcement whatsoever. It can be refused at no cost, and that weakness is named openly rather than disguised as inevitability, because a demand dressed up as a request is a manipulation, and the document would far rather be refusable than dishonest.

So the ask is sized carefully to survive refusal. It is not ‘adopt all of this’; it is a pod — three people, one shared problem, one recorded hour, written down so that the record outranks whatever story anyone later tells about it.
